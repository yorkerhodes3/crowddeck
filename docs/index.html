The DJ booth and the jukebox have never shared one machine.

CrowdDeck is a concept for an open-source venue runtime that runs a professional multi-deck DJ engine and a TouchTunes-style patron request jukebox on a single scheduler, a single library and a single clock — wired together by MIDI so that instruments, controllers and lighting all sit on the same timeline. 01 — THESIS

The gap this project exists to fill

Every product surveyed is excellent at one half of the problem and structurally uninterested in the other half. DJ software assumes a skilled operator is present and gives the audience no voice at all — across djay Pro AI, Serato DJ Pro, rekordbox 7, Traktor Pro 4 and Mixxx, the crowd-participation score is effectively zero. Jukebox platforms assume no operator is present and give the music no craft: they are queue managers with a payment terminal bolted on, and none of them can beatmatch, loop, or run a stem.

Real venues are both, on a schedule. A bar is an unattended jukebox from 11:00 to 21:00 and a DJ room from 21:00 to close. Today that means two systems, two libraries and an awkward physical handover. Nobody has built the machine that does both, and open source has not even attempted it.

The core design decision. Crowd requests must never write directly to the audio output. They land in a staging lane that the DJ can approve, reorder and beatmatch. When no DJ is present, an autonomous mixer drains that same staging lane with beatmatched transitions. One queue, two consumers. That single choice is what allows a jukebox and a DJ rig to coexist instead of fighting for the speakers — and it is the part no existing product, open or closed, implements.

Four findings that shaped the concept

1. Open source has a DJ engine but no venue layer

Mixxx is a genuinely complete DJ application — 7,000+ stars, actively developed, GPL-2.0-or-later, with beatgrids, key detection, sync lock, DVS, Auto DJ and now STEM support in 2.6. There is no comparable open-source implementation of the TouchTunes interaction model. The crowd plane is where the real greenfield work is.

2. The existing open jukeboxes are built on a legal fault line

Festify, Jukestar and the PartyPlay class all depend on a host's consumer Spotify Premium account. That is a personal-use licence, not a public-performance licence. Festify itself has been archived since 2019. Any venue-credible product must be designed so this path is impossible.

3. Licence choice is an architecture decision, made once

The most valuable code to fork (Mixxx) is GPL-2.0-or-later. Ableton Link is GPL-2.0-or-later, JUCE 9 is AGPLv3-or-commercial, Rubber Band is GPL-or-commercial, Essentia is AGPL-3.0. Get the process boundaries wrong at the start and the whole product is copyleft by accident.

4. MIDI 2.0 finally landed on every platform

Windows 11 MIDI Services (2026), macOS CoreMIDI (since 2021) and Linux ALSA (kernel 6.5+, 2023) all carry Universal MIDI Packets natively. A project starting now should be UMP-native — and MIDI-CI Property Exchange means controllers can describe themselves, which could largely automate the mapping problem that every incumbent still solves with hand-written files.

03 — THE JUKEBOX MODEL, DECODED

What TouchTunes actually got right

TouchTunes is not interesting because of its catalog. It is interesting because of a small number of interaction and business mechanics that no open-source project has replicated, and which are worth copying deliberately.

Position in line

The single most-cited feature. Showing a patron exactly where their song sits in the queue is what converts an unbounded, frustrating wait into a bounded, tolerable one. It is cheap to build and it is the difference between a queue people trust and one they abandon.

Paid priority

Fast Pass / Song Upgrade lets a patron spend extra credits to jump the line. This is the core monetisation primitive of the entire industry — and it works precisely because position in line is visible. One feature creates the demand the other one sells.

Non-expiring credit wallet

Credits never expire and can auto-refill. This converts a transaction into a balance, which is a retention mechanic. Layered on top: TouchTunes Rewards points, emoji reactions and a visible social feed of who played what.

Zero-capex venue economics

Hardware is installed free. Revenue comes from per-song fees that already include the negotiated PRO and label licensing, then splits between the venue and the local amusement operator — the venue commonly taking roughly a quarter to a half of net. Licensing bundled into the play fee is the reason venues accept it.

Operator route management

Both TouchTunes and AMI's Co-Pilot let an operator manage many venues remotely: volume, content scheduling, promotions and detailed route reporting. Multi-tenancy is not an enterprise afterthought here — it is the business model.

The cabinet is a venue display

Playdium, Virtuo/Virtuo II and Angelina are modular, cloud-connected units with HD touchscreens, LED lighting, visualisers, and karaoke or photobooth add-ons. The physical presence advertises the service to everyone in the room — a role a QR code and a wall screen can serve for a fraction of the cost.

And what it got wrong — our opening. The TouchTunes cabinet is cloud-dependent by design. When the venue's connection drops, the music stops. A local-first runtime that keeps working with no internet is a real, demonstrable advantage, not just an ideological preference. Pair that with an open API and no telemetry, and the open-source product has three things the incumbent structurally cannot offer.

The licensing reality every venue product must respect

  • The Jukebox License Office closed in 2025. The JLO was the ASCAP/BMI/SESAC joint clearinghouse that sold one economical annual licence covering all three repertories for coin-operated jukeboxes. With it gone, operators must obtain separate annual licences from ASCAP, BMI and SESAC.
  • Venue background-music licensing is separate and additive. Bars and restaurants typically need ASCAP, BMI, SESAC and GMR, with combined annual cost commonly exceeding $1,000 per location depending on capacity, zones, outdoor playback, and whether there is live music, a DJ or karaoke.
  • A DJ record pool is a legitimate ingest path. Services like BPM Supreme and ZIPDJ (roughly $20–25/month) grant professional DJs the right to play downloaded music live.
  • Consumer streaming is not. This is the line the existing open-source jukeboxes cross.

This is research, not legal advice. The design consequence is concrete: the system must be able to answer "may this venue legally play this track right now?" — which means per-track licence class and a per-venue licensing profile are data model concerns from day one, not a later compliance bolt-on.

05 — OPEN-SOURCE LANDSCAPE

What to fork, adopt, study, and refuse

The brief was explicit: do not write code we could have forked. Stars, language, licence and last-push date below were read directly from the GitHub REST API on . Where GitHub reported NOASSERTION, the repository's actual LICENSE file was fetched and read — which is how JUCE 9 turned out to be AGPLv3-or-commercial and miniaudio turned out to be public-domain-or-MIT-0.

The three-line summary

  • Fork Mixxx for the performance engine. It is the only mature open DJ application in existence and rebuilding EngineMixer, SoundManager, the analysers and the DVS path would cost years.
  • Fork Karaoke Eternal for the crowd plane. It is ISC-licensed — meaning we can relicense it freely — and it already implements QR join, multi-room sessions, a dynamic fair queue and a browser-first control surface, which is 70% of the TouchTunes interaction model.
  • Fork Mopidy for content-source abstraction. Apache-2.0, and its entire purpose is a pluggable backend API that normalises many sources behind one interface — exactly the layer we need, with no copyleft consequences.
Everything else is a dependency or a reference. Navidrome should be consumed over the OpenSubsonic API rather than forked, which keeps it in its own process and its GPL-3.0 out of our binary. That pattern — talk to copyleft components over a socket — recurs throughout the architecture.

07 — MIDI & SYSTEMS INTERCONNECTION

The interconnect fabric

The brief treats MIDI as a first-class subsystem — an interface to musical instruments and other content sources, not merely a way to read knobs. That reframing has real consequences: a groovebox becomes a queueable source, and the leader deck becomes a clock master that the whole room follows.

Design position: be UMP-native from day one

Every major OS now carries MIDI 2.0 Universal MIDI Packets natively. More importantly, MIDI-CI Property Exchange gives a JSON-based model for a device to describe its own controls. Every incumbent still solves mapping with hand-authored files; a UMP-native project could make most controllers self-describing. That is a defensible technical lead available only to a project starting now.

Design position: the browser cannot be the MIDI path

Web MIDI is absent from Safari on macOS and iOS with no announced roadmap — which excludes every iPhone — and Chrome 124+ gates the whole API behind a permission prompt over HTTPS. Combined with 20–30 ms desktop and 50–100 ms mobile Web Audio round-trip latency, the browser is a fine surface for patrons and for mapping UI, and an unacceptable one for the performance engine.

Clock topology

  • The leader deck is the single tempo source. It publishes to MIDI Clock (24 PPQN) and to Ableton Link simultaneously, so cabled hardware and wireless peers share one timeline.
  • MIDI Clock carries tempo; MTC carries position. MTC's ~0.6 ms resolution and susceptibility to traffic delay make it a positional reference only, never a beat-accurate trigger.
  • Keep the clock path short. Thru daisy-chains introduce jitter and can create loops.
  • Ableton Link's quantum gives phase alignment for free: an 8-beat loop and a 4-beat loop stay aligned at each 8-beat boundary, and enableStartStopSync quantises transport launches so everything starts together.
08 — PROPOSED ARCHITECTURE

Two planes, one scheduler, one clock

The dashed boundary is a process boundary and a licence boundary at the same time. Copyleft-heavy components live below it and are addressed over IPC or HTTP; the permissive core lives above it.

Why two planes

They have latency budgets three orders of magnitude apart. The deck engine needs sub-10 ms determinism and must be native; the crowd queue is happy at hundreds of milliseconds and should be web. Forcing them into one runtime compromises both.

Why one scheduler

Two queues would mean two sources of truth about what plays next, and the failure mode is the jukebox and the DJ talking over each other. A single queue with a staging lane and two different consumers is the whole trick.

Why one clock

If the leader deck publishes to MIDI Clock and Ableton Link at once, then instruments, lighting, visuals and other apps in the room are all on the same timeline for free — including during autonomous mode, when no human is driving.

Recommendation to carry into SPECIFICATION.md. Adopt a two-licence structure that matches the two-plane architecture. The Performance Plane forks Mixxx and ships as GPL-2.0-or-later, inheriting its engine, analysers and DVS. Everything else — fusion core, jukebox plane, API, clients, content providers, controller mapping format — is Apache-2.0, and talks to the performance plane over a documented IPC boundary. This keeps the valuable fork legal, keeps the crowd/venue layer reusable by anyone, and preserves the option of a non-GPL alternative engine later. The alternative — a single GPL product — is simpler but permanently forecloses embedding the venue layer in other systems.

This repository's own licence is Apache-2.0, covering the research, dashboard and scaffolding only.

The five decisions, and the recommendations on the table.
  1. Licence structure — split Apache-2.0 core + GPL-2.0-or-later engine across a hard IPC boundary. Both Mixxx and Ableton Link turned out to be GPL-2.0-or-later, which removes the compatibility problem that would have made this fragile.
  2. Engine — fork Mixxx, but contract-first, stub second, fork third. Its ControlObjectScript bus already drives the whole engine from a scripting layer, so a socket transport is far less work than a cold extraction.
  3. Paid priority — build the ordering model and credit ledger in v1; ship no payment rails until v1.1.
  4. Deployment — single-venue appliance, venue_id everywhere, multi-venue later as federation over appliances rather than shared-database multi-tenancy. Offline-first (G1) essentially forces this.
  5. Client architecture — thin native engine plus web consoles. Controller input reaches the engine directly, so web UI latency is cosmetic rather than functional.

All five ratified 2026-08-27. Full rationale, confidence levels and what would reverse each one are in DECISIONS.md.

11 — SPECIFICATION

118 requirements, traced to the research

Phase 2 converts the capability set into testable requirements against the five ratified decisions. The first section written was the engine IPC contract, because everything else is built against it and because a contract must be shaped by its consumer rather than by the fork. This table is generated directly from SPECIFICATION.md, so it cannot drift from the document.

CDEP — the engine protocol

Newline-delimited JSON over a local socket, addressing controls by (group, item) exactly as Mixxx's Control bus does. It is self-describing: describe returns every control with a full descriptor, so both the UI and the MIDI mapping target list are generated with no hard-coded engine knowledge. Deliberately arms-length — no shared memory, no GPL-defined structures — because that generality is a licence requirement, not a style choice.

The rule the architecture exists to protect

The audio thread must never block on IPC. If a client stops reading its socket, the engine drops coalesced updates rather than waiting. A stalled browser tab, a crashed fusion core or a wedged network client cannot glitch the room's audio. That single guarantee is why the two planes are separate processes.

The scheduler, in one formula

Votes and paid boosts are two inputs to one ordering function, which is what lets payments arrive in v1.1 with no scheduler change. The aging term is not a nicety — without it an unpopular request never plays, so anti-starvation is a correctness property.

priority_score = (votes × VOTE_WEIGHT) + (boost_units × BOOST_WEIGHT) + age_bonus
age_bonus = floor(minutes_waiting / AGING_INTERVAL) × AGING_WEIGHT
12 — BACKLOG

Sequenced so the risky part comes first

Ordering follows ADR-002 — contract first, stub engine second, Mixxx fork third. The fusion layer has no prior art anywhere, so it is proven against a throwaway stub engine before committing to fork surgery. The stub then stays permanently as the proof that the engine is replaceable, which is the property the licence split depends on.

Traceability is enforced, not asserted. Every story cites the requirements it satisfies, and tools/build-backlog.mjs fails the build if a story references a requirement that does not exist in the specification. All 118 of 118 requirements are currently covered.
Build has started — and it runs. The CDEP contract, a conformant stub engine, the conformance suite, the licence-lint gate, the Unified Scheduler, the venue API with live push, working patron / DJ / display clients, and the MIDI interconnect — identity-stable ports, soft-takeover, mappings that target CDEP, a 24 PPQN clock, and live instruments as queueable sources. 282 tests, 20 conformance checks, zero dependencies, all green in CI. engine/ stays deliberately empty until SPIKE-1, so nothing is audible yet.
The specification's definition of done now runs as a test. §0.4 describes a full trading day in prose; day-in-the-life.test.js executes it. The venue opens unattended and the fallback carries the room, patrons queue and vote, the fair-queue rules refuse a third request and explain why, a DJ takes over at 21:00 without interrupting what is playing, works a controller where soft-takeover stops the volume jumping, cues a patron request from the staging lane, runs a clock so a live instrument locks to the deck, and the venue exports exactly what was performed — with nothing touching the internet all day. Every other test checks one component; that one checks they compose.
SPIKE-1: the assumption everything rests on has been checked — and it held. The whole plan depends on one claim: that Mixxx's control bus is enumerable, self-describing and drivable from outside. Reading the real source confirms it — ControlDoublePrivate::getAllInstances() returns every control, each carrying a name, a description and a default. Better still, EngineMixer includes only QtCore, so the engine is not entangled with the GUI — the single largest risk in "strip Mixxx to headless" simply isn't there.

Looking closely also changed the contract. CDEP required min and max on every control so clients could scale into range. Mixxx cannot supply them — and it turns out clients shouldn't want them: each control carries its own curve, logarithmic for gain, audio-taper for volume, linear for the crossfader. So CDEP now speaks normalised parameter space and the engine applies its own curve. The specification had declared master gain as 0..4 linear; it is really -14..14 dB, so a fader at half travel would have meant "double gain" to a client and "unity" to the engine. A test now pins that down.

But the spike is only half done, and the honest half is missing. Building Mixxx needs Qt6, CMake and MSVC, and measuring latency needs real audio and MIDI hardware — none of which exist in this environment. The §8.1 latency budgets remain unvalidated assumptions, and they are now the largest technical risk in the plan. Read the full report, including what it deliberately did not do.
The venue now remembers. Everything above ran in memory, which meant a restart lost the queue, the credits and the evidence trail. data/ fixes that on Node's built-in node:sqlite — so still nothing to install.

Three decisions are worth calling out because the obvious alternative is subtly wrong in each case. Credits are an append-only ledger, enforced by SQL triggers, not a balance column: a balance has no history, and a rule that lives only in a comment is one careless UPDATE away from rewriting what a patron is owed. A track cannot be stored without a declared licence class — defaulting to unknown would quietly turn "nobody checked" into a stored fact, and unknown is exactly what blocks playback in a commercial venue. And the durable queue stores voter identities, not just vote counts, because a count would let a patron vote again after every reboot — the fairness rule would weaken a little each time the venue restarted.

The play log has no network code at all, which a test enforces by reading the module source and failing if any transport API appears in it. That is the only honest way to keep a no-telemetry promise. Its CSV writer is tested against a track called Say "Hello", Wave Goodbye — one unescaped comma shifts every later column and quietly corrupts a royalty report.

Alongside it, engine-link.js finally delivers the property the whole two-plane split exists for: a core crash does not stop the music. It reconnects with jittered backoff and, crucially, reads the deck before deciding anything — a naive reconnect would re-issue its last load and restart the track the room is dancing to. Tested against a real engine process over a real socket.
