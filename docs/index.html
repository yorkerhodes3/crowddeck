The DJ booth and the jukebox have never shared one machine.

CrowdDeck is a concept for an open-source venue runtime that runs a professional multi-deck DJ engine and a TouchTunes-style patron request jukebox on a single scheduler, a single library and a single clock — wired together by MIDI so that instruments, controllers and lighting all sit on the same timeline. This dashboard is the evidence behind that concept.

01 — THESIS

The gap this project exists to fill

Every product surveyed is excellent at one half of the problem and structurally uninterested in the other half. DJ software assumes a skilled operator is present and gives the audience no voice at all — across djay Pro AI, Serato DJ Pro, rekordbox 7, Traktor Pro 4 and Mixxx, the crowd-participation score is effectively zero. Jukebox platforms assume no operator is present and give the music no craft: they are queue managers with a payment terminal bolted on, and none of them can beatmatch, loop, or run a stem.

Real venues are both, on a schedule. A bar is an unattended jukebox from 11:00 to 21:00 and a DJ room from 21:00 to close. Today that means two systems, two libraries and an awkward physical handover. Nobody has built the machine that does both, and open source has not even attempted it.

The core design decision. Crowd requests must never write directly to the audio output. They land in a staging lane that the DJ can approve, reorder and beatmatch. When no DJ is present, an autonomous mixer drains that same staging lane with beatmatched transitions. One queue, two consumers. That single choice is what allows a jukebox and a DJ rig to coexist instead of fighting for the speakers — and it is the part no existing product, open or closed, implements.

Four findings that shaped the concept

1. Open source has a DJ engine but no venue layer

Mixxx is a genuinely complete DJ application — 7,000+ stars, actively developed, GPL-2.0-or-later, with beatgrids, key detection, sync lock, DVS, Auto DJ and now STEM support in 2.6. There is no comparable open-source implementation of the TouchTunes interaction model. The crowd plane is where the real greenfield work is.

2. The existing open jukeboxes are built on a legal fault line

Festify, Jukestar and the PartyPlay class all depend on a host's consumer Spotify Premium account. That is a personal-use licence, not a public-performance licence. Festify itself has been archived since 2019. Any venue-credible product must be designed so this path is impossible.

3. Licence choice is an architecture decision, made once

The most valuable code to fork (Mixxx) is GPL-2.0-or-later. Ableton Link is GPL-2.0-or-later, JUCE 9 is AGPLv3-or-commercial, Rubber Band is GPL-or-commercial, Essentia is AGPL-3.0. Get the process boundaries wrong at the start and the whole product is copyleft by accident.

4. MIDI 2.0 finally landed on every platform

Windows 11 MIDI Services (2026), macOS CoreMIDI (since 2021) and Linux ALSA (kernel 6.5+, 2023) all carry Universal MIDI Packets natively. A project starting now should be UMP-native — and MIDI-CI Property Exchange means controllers can describe themselves, which could largely automate the mapping problem that every incumbent still solves with hand-written files.

03 — THE JUKEBOX MODEL, DECODED

What TouchTunes actually got right

TouchTunes is not interesting because of its catalog. It is interesting because of a small number of interaction and business mechanics that no open-source project has replicated, and which are worth copying deliberately.

Position in line

The single most-cited feature. Showing a patron exactly where their song sits in the queue is what converts an unbounded, frustrating wait into a bounded, tolerable one. It is cheap to build and it is the difference between a queue people trust and one they abandon.

Paid priority

Fast Pass / Song Upgrade lets a patron spend extra credits to jump the line. This is the core monetisation primitive of the entire industry — and it works precisely because position in line is visible. One feature creates the demand the other one sells.

Non-expiring credit wallet

Credits never expire and can auto-refill. This converts a transaction into a balance, which is a retention mechanic. Layered on top: TouchTunes Rewards points, emoji reactions and a visible social feed of who played what.

Zero-capex venue economics

Hardware is installed free. Revenue comes from per-song fees that already include the negotiated PRO and label licensing, then splits between the venue and the local amusement operator — the venue commonly taking roughly a quarter to a half of net. Licensing bundled into the play fee is the reason venues accept it.

Operator route management

Both TouchTunes and AMI's Co-Pilot let an operator manage many venues remotely: volume, content scheduling, promotions and detailed route reporting. Multi-tenancy is not an enterprise afterthought here — it is the business model.

The cabinet is a venue display

Playdium, Virtuo/Virtuo II and Angelina are modular, cloud-connected units with HD touchscreens, LED lighting, visualisers, and karaoke or photobooth add-ons. The physical presence advertises the service to everyone in the room — a role a QR code and a wall screen can serve for a fraction of the cost.

And what it got wrong — our opening. The TouchTunes cabinet is cloud-dependent by design. When the venue's connection drops, the music stops. A local-first runtime that keeps working with no internet is a real, demonstrable advantage, not just an ideological preference. Pair that with an open API and no telemetry, and the open-source product has three things the incumbent structurally cannot offer.

The licensing reality every venue product must respect

  • The Jukebox License Office closed in 2025. The JLO was the ASCAP/BMI/SESAC joint clearinghouse that sold one economical annual licence covering all three repertories for coin-operated jukeboxes. With it gone, operators must obtain separate annual licences from ASCAP, BMI and SESAC.
  • Venue background-music licensing is separate and additive. Bars and restaurants typically need ASCAP, BMI, SESAC and GMR, with combined annual cost commonly exceeding $1,000 per location depending on capacity, zones, outdoor playback, and whether there is live music, a DJ or karaoke.
  • A DJ record pool is a legitimate ingest path. Services like BPM Supreme and ZIPDJ (roughly $20–25/month) grant professional DJs the right to play downloaded music live.
  • Consumer streaming is not. This is the line the existing open-source jukeboxes cross.

This is research, not legal advice. The design consequence is concrete: the system must be able to answer "may this venue legally play this track right now?" — which means per-track licence class and a per-venue licensing profile are data model concerns from day one, not a later compliance bolt-on.

05 — OPEN-SOURCE LANDSCAPE

What to fork, adopt, study, and refuse

The brief was explicit: do not write code we could have forked. Stars, language, licence and last-push date below were read directly from the GitHub REST API on . Where GitHub reported NOASSERTION, the repository's actual LICENSE file was fetched and read — which is how JUCE 9 turned out to be AGPLv3-or-commercial and miniaudio turned out to be public-domain-or-MIT-0.

The three-line summary

  • Fork Mixxx for the performance engine. It is the only mature open DJ application in existence and rebuilding EngineMixer, SoundManager, the analysers and the DVS path would cost years.
  • Fork Karaoke Eternal for the crowd plane. It is ISC-licensed — meaning we can relicense it freely — and it already implements QR join, multi-room sessions, a dynamic fair queue and a browser-first control surface, which is 70% of the TouchTunes interaction model.
  • Fork Mopidy for content-source abstraction. Apache-2.0, and its entire purpose is a pluggable backend API that normalises many sources behind one interface — exactly the layer we need, with no copyleft consequences.
Everything else is a dependency or a reference. Navidrome should be consumed over the OpenSubsonic API rather than forked, which keeps it in its own process and its GPL-3.0 out of our binary. That pattern — talk to copyleft components over a socket — recurs throughout the architecture.

07 — MIDI & SYSTEMS INTERCONNECTION

The interconnect fabric

The brief treats MIDI as a first-class subsystem — an interface to musical instruments and other content sources, not merely a way to read knobs. That reframing has real consequences: a groovebox becomes a queueable source, and the leader deck becomes a clock master that the whole room follows.

Design position: be UMP-native from day one

Every major OS now carries MIDI 2.0 Universal MIDI Packets natively. More importantly, MIDI-CI Property Exchange gives a JSON-based model for a device to describe its own controls. Every incumbent still solves mapping with hand-authored files; a UMP-native project could make most controllers self-describing. That is a defensible technical lead available only to a project starting now.

Design position: the browser cannot be the MIDI path

Web MIDI is absent from Safari on macOS and iOS with no announced roadmap — which excludes every iPhone — and Chrome 124+ gates the whole API behind a permission prompt over HTTPS. Combined with 20–30 ms desktop and 50–100 ms mobile Web Audio round-trip latency, the browser is a fine surface for patrons and for mapping UI, and an unacceptable one for the performance engine.

Amended by M7 — the second half of that was too strong. The Web MIDI half stands: Safari still has no Web MIDI, so the browser cannot be the interconnect path. But "unacceptable for the performance engine" was an inference from mobile latency figures applied to every case. On a desktop, Web Audio drives two decks, three-band EQ, loops and beatmatched sync well enough to DJ with — measured, not assumed, and now the shipping engine for the standalone deck. It remains unacceptable for scratching, and for a venue appliance that must run headless.

Clock topology

  • The leader deck is the single tempo source. It publishes to MIDI Clock (24 PPQN) and to Ableton Link simultaneously, so cabled hardware and wireless peers share one timeline.
  • MIDI Clock carries tempo; MTC carries position. MTC's ~0.6 ms resolution and susceptibility to traffic delay make it a positional reference only, never a beat-accurate trigger.
  • Keep the clock path short. Thru daisy-chains introduce jitter and can create loops.
  • Ableton Link's quantum gives phase alignment for free: an 8-beat loop and a 4-beat loop stay aligned at each 8-beat boundary, and enableStartStopSync quantises transport launches so everything starts together.
08 — PROPOSED ARCHITECTURE

Two planes, one scheduler, one clock

The dashed boundary is a process boundary and a licence boundary at the same time. Copyleft-heavy components live below it and are addressed over IPC or HTTP; the permissive core lives above it.

Why two planes

They have latency budgets three orders of magnitude apart. The deck engine needs sub-10 ms determinism and must be native; the crowd queue is happy at hundreds of milliseconds and should be web. Forcing them into one runtime compromises both.

Why one scheduler

Two queues would mean two sources of truth about what plays next, and the failure mode is the jukebox and the DJ talking over each other. A single queue with a staging lane and two different consumers is the whole trick.

Why one clock

If the leader deck publishes to MIDI Clock and Ableton Link at once, then instruments, lighting, visuals and other apps in the room are all on the same timeline for free — including during autonomous mode, when no human is driving.

Recommendation to carry into SPECIFICATION.md. Adopt a two-licence structure that matches the two-plane architecture. The Performance Plane forks Mixxx and ships as GPL-2.0-or-later, inheriting its engine, analysers and DVS. Everything else — fusion core, jukebox plane, API, clients, content providers, controller mapping format — is Apache-2.0, and talks to the performance plane over a documented IPC boundary. The five decisions, and the recommendations on the table.
  1. Licence structure — split Apache-2.0 core + GPL-2.0-or-later engine across a hard IPC boundary. Both Mixxx and Ableton Link turned out to be GPL-2.0-or-later, which removes the compatibility problem that would have made this fragile.
  2. Engine — fork Mixxx, but contract-first, stub second, fork third. Its ControlObjectScript bus already drives the whole engine from a scripting layer, so a socket transport is far less work than a cold extraction.
  3. Paid priority — build the ordering model and credit ledger in v1; ship no payment rails until v1.1.
  4. Deployment — single-venue appliance, venue_id everywhere, multi-venue later as federation over appliances rather than shared-database multi-tenancy. Offline-first (G1) essentially forces this.
  5. Client architecture — thin native engine plus web consoles. Controller input reaches the engine directly, so web UI latency is cosmetic rather than functional.

All six ratified 2026-08-27. Full rationale, confidence levels and what would reverse each one are in DECISIONS.md.

11 — SPECIFICATION

123 requirements, traced to the research

Phase 2 converts the capability set into testable requirements against the six ratified decisions. The first section written was the engine IPC contract, because everything else is built against it and because a contract must be shaped by its consumer rather than by the fork. This table is generated directly from SPECIFICATION.md, so it cannot drift from the document.

CDEP — the engine protocol

Newline-delimited JSON over a local socket, addressing controls by (group, item) exactly as Mixxx's Control bus does. It is self-describing: describe returns every control with a full descriptor, so both the UI and the MIDI mapping target list are generated with no hard-coded engine knowledge. Deliberately arms-length — no shared memory, no GPL-defined structures — because that generality is a licence requirement, not a style choice.

The rule the architecture exists to protect

The audio thread must never block on IPC. If a client stops reading its socket, the engine drops coalesced updates rather than waiting. A stalled browser tab, a crashed fusion core or a wedged network client cannot glitch the room's audio. That single guarantee is why the two planes are separate processes.

The scheduler, in one formula

Votes and paid boosts are two inputs to one ordering function, which is what lets payments arrive in v1.1 with no scheduler change. The aging term is not a nicety — without it an unpopular request never plays, so anti-starvation is a correctness property.

priority_score = (votes × VOTE_WEIGHT) + (boost_units × BOOST_WEIGHT) + age_bonus
age_bonus = floor(minutes_waiting / AGING_INTERVAL) × AGING_WEIGHT

This line used to read "nothing is audible yet", because audio was gated behind a Mixxx fork that needs MSVC, Qt6 and CMake. engine/ is still deliberately empty — but audio is no longer waiting on it, because the browser turned out to be a perfectly good audio engine. See M7 below.
M7 — the goal changed, and the blocker evaporated. The brief became concrete: walk into the office on Monday and replace a commercial DJ application, in a browser, on a touchscreen desktop that lies flat. No venue, no MIDI hardware, no native toolchain.

Every remaining path to audio ran through E7, the Mixxx fork — MSVC, Qt6, CMake, hours of first build, none of it available. Then the obvious thing: Web Audio gives sample-accurate scheduling, per-deck gain, biquad filters and playback-rate control. That is a mixer. The blocker was an assumption, not a fact.

It would have been quicker to write a one-off. Writing it against CDEP instead means the scheduler, policy engine and queue drive it unchanged, and it becomes the second independent implementation of the contract — the strongest evidence yet for REQ-LIC-5, that the engine really is replaceable. It also removes the licence question entirely for this path: there is no Mixxx in it, so ADR-001's plane boundary does not even arise.

Measured in a real browser, not asserted: 0.796 peak with the crossfader on deck A and exactly 0.0 on deck B · a 60 Hz tone attenuated 50.2 dB by the bass kill · a 128 BPM track detected as 127.84 · 125 BPM synced to 127.84 leaving a tempo gap of 0 · a 4-beat loop at 119.7 BPM measuring 2.005 s with the source node genuinely looping.
DJX-13 — key lock: the control the engine used to refuse. Moving the pitch fader dragged the musical key with it. At the ±8% a Technics 1200 offers that is up to 1.4 semitones — far enough that a vocal audibly changes key — so any track with a voice in it could not really be beat-matched. Rather than accept the control and ignore it, the engine refused it outright, and said why.

Now measured in a real browser: with the pitch fader at +8%, a 220 Hz tone is heard at 237.6 Hz with key lock off and at exactly 220.0 Hz with it on.

Not a phase vocoder. That is the textbook answer and the wrong one here: it needs an FFT — a dependency, or hundreds of lines to prove — and it smears transients, the one artefact four-to-the-floor material cannot take. Time-domain overlap-add is what SoundTouch, and therefore Mixxx's own key lock, actually uses.

It measured perfectly at 440 Hz while leaving 60 Hz completely uncorrected. The splice search picks the offset that best matches what was already written — and for a grain that has been resampled, the best match is the offset that undoes the resampling. At 440 Hz the search spans several periods and locks to phase, so the shift survives; at 60 Hz one period is 754 samples, the offset slides freely, and the correction is cancelled entirely. Splitting the resampler and the overlap-add into separate stages removes the conflict by construction: a grain copied verbatim cannot be re-pitched by any choice of offset.

Two more, both found by measuring rather than reasoning. The search has to span a whole pitch period — clean splices repeat once per period, so a shorter range can never reach one, and ±128 samples left bass a full semitone sharp. And it only ever needs to look backward, since exactly one clean offset always lies in the period behind: that cut latency from 67ms to 49ms for identical output.

That latency is paid whether key lock is on or off, which looks wasteful and is not. If bypass were free, arming key lock mid-mix would shift that deck 49ms against the other — a flam on every beat, appearing at the exact moment a DJ is least able to diagnose it. A constant delay is learned once; a delay that arrives when you touch a button is a fault.

And one test that passed on broken code. Stereo needs both channels spliced at the same point, or the image wanders. The first version of that test compared the inter-channel delay against its original value with a 6-sample tolerance — then two independent mono shifters, which is exactly what a per-channel decision amounts to, drifted by 4 and passed. The property that actually separates them is stability: the shared decision holds the delay perfectly constant while per-channel wanders by 3 samples.
DJX-14 — key detection, and knowing when not to answer. Key lock holds a track's key while the tempo moves. This answers the other half: what key is it in? Without that there is no harmonic mixing — no way to know the record you are about to bring in clashes with the one already playing. Each deck now shows a Camelot code, and the two badges turn green when the decks mix and red when they do not.

Verified on real audio, not just synthetic: two Internet Archive tracks read as A major (11B) and D major (10B) — one step apart on the wheel — and were correctly shown as compatible.

Goertzel, not an FFT, for the same reason key lock avoided a phase vocoder: an FFT means a dependency or a few hundred lines to prove, and it computes every bin when only 72 are wanted. A Goertzel filter costs about one multiply-add per sample per frequency and lines the bins up with semitones rather than a linear grid — which matters, because uniform FFT spacing is far too coarse in the bass to separate adjacent semitones.

It refuses to guess, on two gates, and both were needed. A tonality gate rejects flat chroma: measured, tonal material scores 2.7–3.3 while drums and noise score 1.20–1.32, so the 1.6 threshold sits in the gap rather than on top of either population. A margin gate then rejects answers where two keys fit about equally well. The second is not redundant — a single sustained note scores 11.07 on tonality, far above the threshold, and is still refused, because one note fits a dozen keys within 0.012. Musically that is the right answer: a note does not establish a key, a progression does.

This is the same discipline the tempo detector learned when autocorrelation confidently reported 150 BPM for a sine tone. The failure that matters is not being wrong — it is being confidently wrong, because a DJ who trusts a key badge will mix two records that clash and not know why. Stated plainly: this is classical chroma matching, not a modern learned model, and real tracks scored 1.83 against that 1.6 gate — a narrower margin than the synthetic tests suggest.

And a test of mine measured nothing while looking fine. The first real-track check waited for analysis.a.duration > 0, which was already true from an earlier synthetic load — so it returned instantly and reported stale data. Caught only because the confidence matched the previous run to seventeen decimal places.
DJX-15 — a bug the new feature created. Key lock's insert delays a deck by a constant 49ms, and the browser buffers on top of that: measured, 59ms total. But the waveform playhead, the platter rotation and the position readout were all drawn from where the source node had read to, not from where the sound was — so the display ran ahead of the music by roughly an eighth of a beat at 128 BPM. Visible on a waveform, and exactly the kind of small wrongness that makes an application feel untrustworthy without anyone being able to say why.

This was only worth fixing once key lock existed. The browser's own latency alone was small enough to ignore; adding 49ms to it was not. Adding a feature moved the threshold on an existing imprecision — the compromise was fine until it wasn't.

Corrected only while playing: a paused deck emits nothing, so its playhead should show where it will resume from, and subtracting there would make cueing appear to set the cue point early. Clamped at zero, because the first 59ms is the case where the correction exceeds the position itself.
DJX-16 — record the mix, and the arithmetic that chose the format. The last thing the reference application did that this did not. A practice set you cannot keep is worth much less than one you can — and recording is the only honest way to hear your own mixing, since in the moment you are concentrating on the next track rather than on the blend you just made.

Verified by decoding it back, not by checking the file exists: a 3.84s stereo 48 kHz recording came back with a fundamental of exactly 330.0 Hz — the tone that was playing. A file of the right size can still be silence.

MediaRecorder rather than writing a WAV, and the arithmetic decides it. Stereo 48 kHz float is about 11 MB per minute, so a two-hour set is 1.3 GB held in a browser tab. That does not degrade gracefully — it crashes the tab, and it crashes it at the end of a long set, which is the worst possible moment to lose a recording. Compressed chunks cost tens of megabytes an hour. Lossy but it survives is the right way round for something whose entire purpose is not to lose your set.

It taps; it does not sit in the path. Connected in parallel with the speakers and downstream of the limiter, so it captures what was actually heard. In series, a recording failure would become an audio failure — and silence in front of people is far worse than a lost file.

Three smaller decisions, each guarding a silent failure. Chunks are requested every second rather than only at the end, so a tab that dies mid-set leaves something recoverable. Empty chunks are discarded, because some players stop at the first zero-length block rather than skipping it — turning a good recording into a silent one. And filenames carry local time to the second, so two takes a minute apart cannot quietly overwrite each other.
DJX-19 — a measurement that killed the cheap design. Sourcing records by tempo against what is already playing. The compatibility question sounds like a tolerance to invent — ±5 BPM, or ±10% — and it is not: the deck already defines it exactly. A record can be beat-matched only if the pitch fader can physically reach it, which is ±8%. So this asks syncRate, the same function the SYNC button uses. A record this list calls mixable is one SYNC will genuinely lock.

Verified live against a playing 138.88 BPM deck: 22 of 25 results scanned, 8 mixable, ranked +0.0% · +1.2% · −1.2% · −2.5% · +3.7% — ascending by how far the fader must travel — with unmixable rows dimmed and pushed below.

The cheap version would have been nearly free, and was wrong. A 256 KB byte-range prefix decodes in 350 ms and yields 32 seconds of audio; the Archive supports Range with CORS. Measured against full-file analysis on ten releases, it disagreed on five — the opening of a record is frequently an intro with a different feel. A window from the middle was no better: three agreed, five disagreed, two would not decode at all. And confidence did not separate the good answers from the bad — one wrong tempo scored 0.95.

So there was no honest way to show a preview-derived figure and flag which ones to trust. A browse list showing a confidently wrong BPM is worse than one showing nothing: it sends a DJ to a record that will not lock, and the discovery happens in front of people. It therefore pays the bandwidth and computes the same figure the deck computes on load — opt-in, four at a time (twenty-wide would starve the audio thread while music is playing), stoppable, and skipping anything over 12 MB rather than pulling a 60 MB DJ mix to browse it. Results are ranked, never filtered: a list that empties itself is worse than one that puts the good matches on top.

And then a defect found by asking whether the ranking follows the decks. It did not: the list was drawn on search and on scan completion, but never on track load — so after loading a 140 BPM record the header still read "under 100.0 BPM" and still recommended the 100 BPM track. An actively wrong recommendation rather than a missing one. The fix is split on purpose: loading a different record re-sorts, because the question "what should I play next" has genuinely changed, while moving the pitch fader updates only the numbers — re-sorting on every frame of a fader drag would make the rows jump under the finger dragging them. Both halves verified: a new record flipped the order, a +4% fader move held it and moved the labels from +0.0 / −2.3 / +3.2 to +4.0 / +1.6 / +7.4.
DJX-17 / DJX-18 — the waveform became usable, and the browse list became informative. The waveform showed where you were but could not take you anywhere. Now a tap or drag moves the playhead: verified in a browser, a click at 25% of a 100-second track lands at 25.0s, at 75% lands at 75.0s, and a drag past the right edge clamps rather than running off the end. Seeking applies continuously during the drag, because scrubbing to find a drop is the point — a deck that only moves when you let go cannot be used to search a track by ear.

And a bug that hid inside a convenience. setPointerCapture throws InvalidPointerId for a pointer the browser no longer considers active, and it was being called before the seek — so a failed capture silently swallowed the seek itself. The seek now happens first and capture is best-effort, which is the right order regardless: capture is an enhancement for the drag, the seek is the actual function.

The browse list now carries genre tags, play count and size — 24 of 25 rows tagged in a live search. The tags come from the Archive's subject field, which is also what the search reads, so showing it finally explains why a record came back for the word typed.
DJX-20 — "can we use YouTube?" No, and here is the evidence. It was a reasonable ask: that is where the music is. The answer is no on two independent grounds, both verified rather than asserted.

1. YouTube's own Terms forbid the use case by name. "Permissions and Restrictions", clause 9: "use the Service to view or listen to Content other than for personal, non-commercial use (for example, you may not publicly screen videos or stream music from the Service)". DJing is publicly streaming music from the Service. That rules out the permitted embed too — this is not merely a point about downloaders.

2. It could not work anyway. Measured in a real browser: fetch of a watch page is CORS-blocked; the official IFrame embed's contentDocument is null; and createMediaElementSource throws a TypeError when handed an iframe. Web Audio can never see the samples — so no crossfader, no EQ, no key lock, no BPM, no waveform, no recording. A video player beside a deck, not a deck.

REQ-CON-7 already encoded this and the build check enforces it — proven by wiring a YouTube URL in and watching check-content-sources exit 1.

So the legitimate library got bigger instead. Openverse — the WordPress Foundation's Creative Commons search — is now a second source, searched concurrently with the Archive and merged. It reaches Jamendo's catalogue without an API key, which the Archive-only library could not. Verified end to end: 45 rows from one search (25 Archive + 20 Openverse) and a Jamendo track loaded and played — "Fantasy - Techno", 314s, 140.63 BPM, key 5A, zero console errors.

The licence filter is the point. An unfiltered Openverse search is mostly unusable here: measured on "techno", the default response carried by-nc-nd, by-nc-sa and by-nc — every one non-commercial and unplayable in a venue. As with the Archive, the upstream filter is not trusted alone; every result is classified again by the same cc-licence.js the venue policy engine uses. Proven by deleting that second filter and watching the test fail.

And the bug that cost the most time had nothing to do with any of it. Openverse caps anonymous requests at page_size=20 and answers 401 for anything larger — not 400, not 429. The app asked for 25 to match the Archive's page, so every Openverse search failed while every hand-written probe, which used smaller numbers, worked. It looked exactly like intermittent rate limiting and was a hard boundary: 20 returns 200, 21 returns 401.
DJX-22 to DJX-25 — sources, Automix, Autotune, and the honest answer about DJ catalogues.

The provider model already existed; the deck was not using it. providers/src/provider.js has defined search / resolve / streamUrl / licenceClass since M3 per REQ-CON-5, with a router that fans queries out and reports failures rather than silently serving a smaller catalogue. Meanwhile the deck had grown its own Archive client, then its own Openverse client, then a hand-rolled merge — two source mechanisms, one tested and one not. BrowserProvider now extends Provider: the same contract, verified in a browser. Adding a source is one adapter plus one registry line.

Which catalogues do commercial DJ apps actually use, and can we? Serato, rekordbox, djay Pro, Traktor and VirtualDJ pull from Beatport LINK, Beatsource, TIDAL, SoundCloud Go+, Apple Music and — since September 2025 — Spotify again. Every one of those is a negotiated commercial agreement, not an open API. DJ use involves mixing, which triggers different licensing than passive streaming, and no public API grants it. The one genuine exception is Apple MusicKit JS, a public browser SDK where each listener brings their own subscription — worth investigating, with the obvious risk that DRM-protected playback almost certainly cannot be routed into Web Audio, which is the same wall YouTube hit.

Does Openverse need an API key? No — and one could not be used here anyway. A registered app would raise limits from 20/min and 200/day to 100/min and 10,000/day, and the page cap from 20 to 50 (verified from Openverse's source; documentation claiming 500 is wrong). But registration issues a confidential client and the token exchange requires the client_secret — there is no PKCE or public-client flow, so putting it in a page would publish it. The code accepts a token but never mints one. Anonymous is genuinely adequate: 200 searches a day is far more than an evening needs.

Spoken word, as asked for. LibriVox — 21,761 public-domain recordings — is now a source; a search for "history" returns 40 rows, all public domain. Its own API is CORS-blocked, but the Archive mirrors it. Its licence basis is weaker and the contract now forces that to be stated: these items carry no per-item licence URL, so the provider declares collection_policy and must cite the policy it relies on.

Two catalogues were rejected on the evidence. The Great 78 Project — 187,031 digitised 78s — was checked rather than assumed: Universal and Sony sued the Internet Archive over it in 2023, it settled confidentially in September 2025, and contested works were removed. That is not something to put in front of a venue. Old-time radio is overwhelmingly by-nc, which the policy engine blocks, so it would simply look broken.

Caching was decided by measurement. The obvious cache holds decoded audio — measured, that is a trap: decoded is 9–44× larger than encoded (three tracks = 137 MB vs 8.8 MB) while fetching is the slow part at 1–3.7 s against ~1 s to decode. So it caches encoded bytes, LRU, bounded in bytes rather than entries. And decodeAudioData detaches its input, so a cache returning its own instance goes silent on the second play — guarded, and the guard initially tested nothing because it detached a cache miss while the bug lives on the hit path.

Automix hands the set over: verified live, the crossfade ran 8.8 s → 0.5 s remaining, the incoming deck started and the fader reached 0.91. Beat-matching happens before the incoming deck is audible, because changing tempo while a track is heard is a pitch slide. And manual input always wins — touch the fader and automix stops touching it, because a control that fights the hand on it is worse than no automatic control.

Autotune proved by re-measuring rather than by inspecting a number: a tone 40 cents sharp of A4 detected at 450.0 Hz came out at exactly 440.0 Hz. It tunes to the key of whichever deck is playing. Two constraints are stated rather than hidden — ~90–110 ms of latency makes it fine in the mix and unusable as a headphone monitor, and a mic plus loudspeakers is a feedback loop no software can fix.
Five bugs that only appeared when the thing was played. Every one was invisible to reading and to unit tests, and every one would otherwise have been found on stage.

A sine tone was detected as 150 BPM. Autocorrelation always has a maximum, so it reports a confident tempo for material with no beat at all. Worse, the peak turns out to be inversely related to whether a beat exists — measured, a pure tone scored 1.00 and a real 128 BPM click track 0.66, because a drone's onset envelope is tiny numerical ripple that is itself highly periodic. Gating on it would have rejected every real beat and accepted every drone. The discriminator is the onset envelope's crest factor: 29–55 for beats, 3–6 for tones and noise, so the threshold sits in an empty gap between two populations.

The whole display froze while the audio kept playing. requestAnimationFrame does not fire in a page that is not being composited, which stopped the playhead, the meters and the cue lights dead. A frozen playhead is worse than no playhead, because it still looks live. A watchdog now drives the render when rAF goes quiet — and the first version of that watchdog guarded on document.hidden, so it did nothing in exactly the case it existed for.

Also: rapid EQ moves were being misread as double-tap-to-reset and snapping the band back to unity mid-mix; a fixed 300 px mixer column pushed deck B's transport off the screen edge; and EQ unity sat at 25% of fader travel, so a neutral EQ looked cut — on hardware neutral is the centre detent, and that is what a hand expects.
The specification's definition of done now runs as a test. §0.4 describes a full trading day in prose; day-in-the-life.test.js executes it. The venue opens unattended and the fallback carries the room, patrons queue and vote, the fair-queue rules refuse a third request and explain why, a DJ takes over at 21:00 without interrupting what is playing, works a controller where soft-takeover stops the volume jumping, cues a patron request from the staging lane, runs a clock so a live instrument locks to the deck, and the venue exports exactly what was performed — with nothing touching the internet all day. Every other test checks one component; that one checks they compose.
SPIKE-1: the assumption everything rests on has been checked — and it held. The whole plan depends on one claim: that Mixxx's control bus is enumerable, self-describing and drivable from outside. Reading the real source confirms it — ControlDoublePrivate::getAllInstances() returns every control, each carrying a name, a description and a default. Better still, EngineMixer includes only QtCore, so the engine is not entangled with the GUI — the single largest risk in "strip Mixxx to headless" simply isn't there.

Looking closely also changed the contract. CDEP required min and max on every control so clients could scale into range. Mixxx cannot supply them — and it turns out clients shouldn't want them: each control carries its own curve, logarithmic for gain, audio-taper for volume, linear for the crossfader. So CDEP now speaks normalised parameter space and the engine applies its own curve. The specification had declared master gain as 0..4 linear; it is really -14..14 dB, so a fader at half travel would have meant "double gain" to a client and "unity" to the engine. A test now pins that down.

But the spike is only half done, and the honest half is missing. Building Mixxx needs Qt6, CMake and MSVC, and measuring latency needs real audio and MIDI hardware — none of which exist in this environment. The §8.1 latency budgets remain unvalidated assumptions, and they are now the largest technical risk in the plan. Read the full report, including what it deliberately did not do.
The venue now remembers. Everything above ran in memory, which meant a restart lost the queue, the credits and the evidence trail. data/ fixes that on Node's built-in node:sqlite — so still nothing to install.

Three decisions are worth calling out because the obvious alternative is subtly wrong in each case. Credits are an append-only ledger, enforced by SQL triggers, not a balance column: a balance has no history, and a rule that lives only in a comment is one careless UPDATE away from rewriting what a patron is owed. A track cannot be stored without a declared licence class — defaulting to unknown would quietly turn "nobody checked" into a stored fact, and unknown is exactly what blocks playback in a commercial venue. And the durable queue stores voter identities, not just vote counts, because a count would let a patron vote again after every reboot — the fairness rule would weaken a little each time the venue restarted.

The play log has no network code at all, which a test enforces by reading the module source and failing if any transport API appears in it. That is the only honest way to keep a no-telemetry promise. Its CSV writer is tested against a track called Say "Hello", Wave Goodbye — one unescaped comma shifts every later column and quietly corrupts a royalty report.

Alongside it, engine-link.js finally delivers the property the whole two-plane split exists for: a core crash does not stop the music. It reconnects with jittered backoff and, crucially, reads the deck before deciding anything — a naive reconnect would re-issue its last load and restart the track the room is dancing to. Tested against a real engine process over a real socket.
The licence question is now a decision, not an open item. ADR-006 records four owner choices: two separate downloads, US and EU, single-GPL accepted as a fallback, and no counsel engaged.

Separate downloads is the part that matters. It means the project never distributes a combined work at all — an Apache-2.0 venue layer that contains no Mixxx code, and a GPL engine, shipped as distinct artifacts. Whatever combining happens, happens on the operator's machine.

Given that, keeping the split costs nothing. We hold copyright on every line of the Apache layer and can relicense it to GPL whenever we like, so the worst case of being wrong is the fallback — which is exactly where going single-GPL today would have landed us anyway. Abandoning it, by contrast, is a one-way door: once outside contributors commit to a GPL venue layer, going back needs all of their permissions. So the option is free and closing it is not.

The realistic threat is not a courtroom, it is a well-meaning convenience installer. Hence REQ-LIC-8 — artifacts MUST stay separate — and REQ-LIC-9, a DCO keeping the fallback exercisable.

Stated plainly: no legal review was obtained and none is planned. This is knowing risk acceptance with a cheap remedy, not a clearance, and it should not be read as one. The review pack stays ready with seven precise questions should that change.
CI was green while 76 tests were not running. The workflow inlined its own copy of the test glob list instead of calling npm test. Three directories were added to package.json over time and the workflow never caught up, so every persistence test, every QR test and every licence-lint test ran locally and never executed in CI — 229 running where 305 should have.

That is the worst shape this failure can take: the signal was not merely absent, it was actively reassuring. The fix is not to update the list but to delete it — CI now calls npm scripts only, and ci-workflow.test.js fails if the duplication comes back. A second source of truth that nothing reconciles will always drift eventually.

Alongside it, REQ-LIC-8 stopped being a sentence and became a check: release.json declares the two artifacts and check-artifacts.mjs fails if either contains both planes, reusing the licence-lint prefixes so the two cannot disagree about where the boundary is. A DCO job covers REQ-LIC-9. Both were verified by reintroducing the exact mistake they exist to catch.
Two decisions that a boolean cannot express.

VEN-3 — what "the venue has a PRO licence" actually means. In the US a venue needs separate licences from ASCAP, BMI and SESAC, and since Global Music Rights began signing major writers away from the incumbents, a fourth. Holding three of four is not 75% covered — it is fully exposed on whatever the fourth controls. The interesting case is the common one: the track carries no PRO metadata and the venue holds some but not all licences. Blocking would block most of the catalogue and get the feature switched off; allowing silently manufactures false confidence. So the assessment returns coverage: "gap" alongside allowed: true, naming the missing PROs — the venue can buy the licence or accept a known risk knowingly, but the system never claims a track is cleared when it does not know.

Replacing the hard-coded holdsPro: true in the API changed which tracks patrons can see — and the entire suite stayed green, because nothing exercised licence gating through the API. A default governing a legal question should not be changeable without a test objecting, so tests now object.

SPIKE-2 — the harness, before the hardware. spike/spike-2/ is written: a probe whose audio callback reads a clock into a preallocated array and does nothing else, because a probe that calls printf() in the callback measures printf(). The analysis half is finished and tested today, with no audio toolchain, on synthetic data whose correct answer is known — which is the only way to catch an off-by-one percentile, since real data gives you nothing to check against. Ranking is on p99 and maximum, never the mean; any xrun disqualifies a configuration outright; and "too close to call" is a permitted verdict rather than a coin flip dressed up as a number.
The latency budget is no longer an assumption. §8.1 asked for a sub-10 ms audio callback and, until now, nobody had measured whether that was reachable — it was the largest open technical risk in the plan. It is reachable: WASAPI shared at 128 frames delivered a p99 callback interval of 3.667 ms on an ordinary laptop, with 0.14% late callbacks.

Three things the measurement taught that reasoning had not. Exclusive mode was decisively worse than shared — 17% late callbacks against 0.14% — which inverts the received wisdom, so share mode must be configurable rather than hard-coded. The first run measured a USB speakerphone, because that was the Windows default output; its large hardware buffer would have been read as "no configuration met the budget", and the budget might have been loosened to accommodate a conference phone. And the xrun check was too strict: it failed a run on any late callback, when with two periods of buffering a single late callback is not a glitch. It now judges a rate, with the threshold set from the measured populations rather than from taste.

Built with a portable GCC toolchain, since MSVC needs administrator rights this machine does not have. That is stated rather than hidden — though a 10 ms budget dominated by the OS audio stack is not something a compiler choice moves. ASIO, CoreAudio and ALSA remain unmeasured and need hardware or platforms that aren't here.
The flaw that makes existing open-source jukeboxes unshippable, designed out. Several are well-built and legally unusable in a venue: they play a member of staff's personal Spotify account through a PA system. Every consumer streaming licence forbids public performance in plain terms, and no amount of PRO licensing fixes it — a venue can hold every ASCAP, BMI, SESAC and GMR licence going and still breach the service's terms. Two different permissions, two different parties.

So REQ-CON-7 is a build check rather than a paragraph. check-content-sources.mjs fails on any import of a consumer-streaming client or a downloader, and on any source reference to their API hosts. The rule is not "no streaming" — licensed B2B services that sell public-performance rights are exactly what a venue should use, and remain welcome, as do OpenSubsonic, Creative Commons repertoire and record pools.

It is enforced because "we decided not to integrate Spotify" is precisely the kind of decision that erodes: someone wires up a downloader one evening because it genuinely improves the demo, nobody remembers the reasoning, and a load-bearing property quietly stops being true.

Writing the tests found the guard was silently inert. The comment stripper treated the // in https:// as a line comment, so every banned host inside a URL was invisible — it would have passed a codebase full of Spotify calls. Reading the regex would not have shown that; constructing the violation did.
One interface for every source, and a dead source can't take the venue with it. CON-1 lands the provider contract — search, resolve, stream URL, licence class — shaped after Mopidy's backend API, with two deliberate differences.

licenceClass is part of the contract, with no default. A provider that cannot establish a licence returns unknown, which policy blocks in a commercial venue. That looks pedantic until you picture the alternative: a provider omits the field, a router fills in something reasonable, and a venue publicly performs music nobody ever checked. "Nobody checked" and "checked and it's fine" must never be the same value.

Providers are searched concurrently, under a timeout. A router that awaited each in turn would hand back the whole no-WAN guarantee — one unreachable remote service and search hangs, taking the local library down with it, which is exactly the cloud-jukebox failure this product exists to avoid. Failures are returned as { tracks, errors, degraded } rather than swallowed, so the console can say "Jamendo is unreachable, showing 3 of 4 sources". A partial answer labelled as partial is useful; a partial answer presented as complete is a lie the software is telling.

Wiring it in exposed a one-line mistake that broke every API request: the option was called router, and the API class already used this.router for its HTTP route table — so assigning a provider router replaced the routing wholesale and every endpoint returned 500. The names are now distinct and a test pins them apart, checking the endpoint end to end rather than just the wiring.
A fresh install now has music it may lawfully play. An appliance that arrives with an empty catalogue cannot be evaluated — you cannot tell whether the queue, the fairness rules or the crossfade work until something is playing. CON-5 ships a Jamendo provider, so the first ten minutes are "it works" rather than "now import your library".

The interesting part is not the HTTP client, it is deciding what a Creative Commons licence actually permits — the most legally consequential parsing in the product, so it lives in its own module and is exhaustively tested. The rule in one line: if the licence contains nc, a commercial venue may not perform it. That is the whole of the commercial question. nd and sa constrain derivative works, not performance, so an unmodified playback is unaffected by either.

That distinction matters because the two possible errors are not symmetric. Treating by-nd as unsafe would discard a large slice of legitimately playable catalogue for no legal reason; treating by-nc as safe would be a breach. Anything not positively recognised becomes unknown, which policy blocks. Non-commercial tracks are filtered twice — server-side and client-side — because a silent API change would otherwise put unplayable music in front of patrons, and that consequence lands on the venue rather than on us.

One API behaviour was worth handling carefully: Jamendo signals failure inside a 200 response, so checking the HTTP status alone would turn an invalid client ID into a silently empty catalogue — a venue with a search box that never finds anything and no explanation. Probing the guards also found a test passing for an incidental reason: it checked one shape of lookalike host and missed the case where the real domain is embedded in a path. Now all three shapes are covered.
Speaking Subsonic — and finding that a requirement cited an extension that does not exist. API-2 exposes an OpenSubsonic surface, which buys an existing client ecosystem on day one. The fit is unusually good: Subsonic's jukeboxControl means "playback on the server's own audio hardware", which is exactly what this product is.

REQ-API-11 required "the jukeboxMediaTypes extension". There is no such extension. The registry defines exactly ten, and that is not among them — the name came from concept-phase research and was never verified, the same failure that put non-existent projects in the first Tavily pass. The requirement's intent was sound and is kept: a stock jukebox playlist is a list of library song IDs, and this queue contains live MIDI instruments and CC provider tracks that are not library songs. Inventing a private field and implying it was a standard would have been worse than the original error, so it ships as crowddeck.mediaTypes — unmistakably ours — and a test asserts every non-namespaced extension we advertise is a real, published one.

Subsonic clients are staff, because the Subsonic auth model has no patron concept and mapping one to a patron would let it walk around credit limits. That has a consequence: an md5(password + salt) credential can skip and clear. So the surface is off until an operator gives it a password of its own, and refuses to start if that is the staff key. Staff are exempt from the patron quota but never from policy — fairness is a courtesy rule, licensing is a legal control that binds the venue whoever pressed the button.

Building it surfaced three bugs. Queue removal used skip(), but skipped is only reachable from cued or playing — so every clear silently failed, and skip() also records the track in recentPlays, which would have put a track nobody heard into cooldown. And a cross-module unit mismatch: providers and the data layer emit milliseconds while CDEP documents load.duration in seconds, so a 245,000 ms track was loaded as sixty-eight hours and the deck never reached the end. Nothing threw — which is exactly why it survived — and all three clients were quietly formatting the wrong unit too. One conversion now sits at the boundary, and tests pin it in the engine, the clients and the queue.
Inheriting the library the venue already has — and the question the protocol can't answer. Plenty of venues already run Navidrome, Gonic, Astiga or Airsonic, and CON-4 speaks their Subsonic API so the appliance adopts that curated, already-paid-for catalogue instead of demanding it be imported again. It is a network client to a separate process, which is also what keeps Navidrome's GPL-3.0 out of our binary.

The difficult part isn't HTTP. A Subsonic server is somebody else's library, and the protocol says nothing about what rights the operator holds in the files. Three options, and only one survives contact: inferring a licence from tags is guessing, which the whole design forbids; marking everything unknown is honest but ships the provider dead, since policy blocks unknown in a commercial venue. So the operator declares it — a required argument with no default. They ripped the CDs, or they pay the record pool. They know, we cannot, and they carry the liability. Every track then carries an attestation naming who declared it and when. If a performing-rights body ever asks why the venue believed it could play something, "a named person on a date" is an answer. "The software assumed so" is not.

The second concern is that Subsonic authenticates in the query string — including the stream URL, which is handed to the audio engine and is exactly the kind of string that ends up in a log line or a crash report. So errors name the method and host but never quote the URL, stream URLs are minted per call and never cached, and sending a plaintext password to a non-TLS host that isn't loopback simply throws: on a venue's shared Wi-Fi that is a credential handed to the room.

Writing the test for that exemption found a real vulnerability in it. The check had been "does the hostname start with 127." — so an attacker who controls evil.test publishes 127.0.0.1.evil.test, and the venue's password is sent there in cleartext. It now parses a full IPv4 literal. Ten of the 51 tests run against a real socket, where the server recomputes md5(password + salt) itself and rejects a mismatch — swapping the concatenation order turns six of them red, which is the assurance a stubbed fetch can never give. That probe also exposed a flaw in the harness: a failing test hung rather than failing, because one server was closed outside a finally. A red test that hangs CI is worse than one that reports.
Controllers that map themselves — and reading the actual spec instead of trusting a search. Every incumbent DJ application ships hand-authored mapping files, one per controller, maintained forever. MID-7 implements MIDI-CI Property Exchange so a capable device describes its own controls and needs no file. It is the clearest lead in the plan over the closed products.

The wire format is taken from the primary documents — M2-101-UM (MIDI-CI v1.2) Appendix D for the Sub-ID#2 assignments, Table 33 for the chunked Get Property Data layout. That care was immediately warranted. A web search stated plainly that 0x35 was Set Property Data. It is Reply to Get Property Data; 0x36 is Set. Building on that would have produced a client that talked past every real device while looking entirely correct, so the tests now assert those constants longhand rather than deriving them from the encoder — a test that builds its expectation from the code it tests proves only self-consistency, and self-consistency with a wrong constant is the failure mode in question.

The controller-list resource is deliberately not hard-coded, because neither document defines one. Coming straight off a requirement that cited an extension which never existed, inventing a schema here would have been the same mistake twice. It is discovered from the device's own ResourceList, and any control whose type or number cannot be confidently interpreted produces no binding at all — returned in skipped with a reason. In a venue, a fader silently bound to the wrong deck control is far worse than a fader that is not bound yet: an unmapped control costs a five-second MIDI-learn, a mis-mapped one costs a mistake in front of people.

REQ-MIDI-9 — the human always wins — is why merging happens per physical control, not per file. A DJ who re-binds one knob keeps the other forty-nine the device described; lose those and nobody would use auto-mapping again. And a user binding replaces the auto one rather than joining it, or a single knob would drive two engine controls at once.
Music on first run, a record that spins, and a straight answer about MIDI over Wi-Fi. Three things the deck was missing, and one question worth answering properly.

The library was the real gap. A DJ application with nothing in it cannot be evaluated — you cannot tell whether the crossfader, the loops or the beatgrid work until something is playing, and asking someone to import their collection first is the wrong order. The constraint that decided the source turned out to be CORS, tested from a real browser rather than assumed: the Internet Archive's search, metadata and audio all pass and decode; ccMixter sends no Access-Control-Allow-Origin at all, so it is unusable from a page; Jamendo and the Free Music Archive need API keys and stay server-side where a credential can be held. Every result is classified again on the way out by the same Creative Commons module the venue side uses, because the Archive's licence field is operator-supplied and unvalidated — and the licence is shown on every row, since it is the fact that decides whether a track may be performed.

Using it found two things reading it would not. Searching title and creator alone returned zero results for "chiptune"; including subject and description returned 66 — genre is how a DJ searches, and on the Archive genre lives in the subject tags. And a 30 MB download behind a button reading "…" is indistinguishable from a broken button, so the load now reports percentage, then decode, then analyse.

The platter is not decoration. It answers three questions at a glance that no number does: is this deck moving at all, roughly how fast, and where in the revolution the beat sits. Rotation comes from the deck's own playhead, never from wall-clock time — so the record turns because the music is playing, slows with the pitch fader and stops dead on pause. The arithmetic lives in a tested module rather than in the page, because a cue jump and a loop wrap are both discontinuities in position and neither is rotation: turning by them would fling the record round, and a backward cue would spin it the wrong way. Three visual bugs were found by looking at it — the platter escaped its row and covered the track title, hiding it on short screens collapsed the EQ faders with it, and the first version was too subtle to read from a metre away, which defeats the point of having one.

Then the artwork went on, and a much worse bug fell out of it. A previous note here claimed the Archive had no reliable cover art. Checking rather than repeating it found sleeves on most releases — and the CORS block on the Archive's image endpoint turned out to be irrelevant, because an <img> needs CORS only if you read the pixels back, and nothing does. With a visible label finally on the record, it became obvious that the disc was swinging around a point outside itself rather than spinning in place — measured, 103px right and 30px up. The cause: CSS transform-origin: 50% 50% resolves to 100 CSS pixels, while SVG's three-argument rotate(angle 100 100) uses 100 user units, which render at about 78px. The two combined into a translation. The CSS rule was redundant — the SVG rotate already carries its own centre — and removing it fixed it. A plain dark disc had hidden the whole thing.
Does MIDI 2.0 work over Wi-Fi? Yes — and you still should not put a clock on it. Network MIDI 2.0 is real and ratified: M2-124-UM, November 2024, UDP carrying Universal MIDI Packets with forward error correction. The older RTP-MIDI (RFC 6295) is MIDI 1.0 only — its abstract says so in as many words. Neither is the problem.

The problem is that MIDI clock is untimestamped. Twenty-four pulses per quarter note, one every 20.83 ms at 120 BPM, and the receiver fires on arrival — so it cannot distinguish "that pulse was late" from "the tempo changed". Casual Wi-Fi jitter is 4–20 ms, rising to ~32 ms average with spikes near 500 ms once a laptop enables power save. The timing error is the same order of magnitude as the interval being measured, and one to two orders worse than this project's own 1 ms budget.

MIDI 2.0's Jitter Reduction timestamps (32 µs) would fix that in principle — but Network MIDI 2.0 v1.0 defines no time-synchronisation mechanism at all, and timestamps are useless without a shared clock. Ableton Link avoids the problem rather than solving it: it shares tempo and beat phase as state, so each device computes its position from its own high-resolution clock and a late packet costs nothing.

So the position is: clock on a cable, wireless tempo over Link, Network MIDI 2.0 for control traffic where tens of milliseconds are tolerable. Also worth knowing — no platform ships native Network MIDI 2.0 over Wi-Fi today. Windows MIDI Services ships the MIDI 2.0 stack with network support announced but unreleased; Linux has a userland reference implementation; macOS and iOS ship RTP-MIDI, which is MIDI 1.0.
The queue was fair by every rule it had, and still felt rigged. CRW-1 is the one story with a FORK verdict, and the question was what Karaoke Eternal actually contributes when the Unified Scheduler already has a fair queue. The answer is the single property a pure score sort cannot produce: rotation.

Two entries from one patron can outscore the room and play back to back, while somebody who queued once waits behind both. No rule is broken — staff-pinned first, then score, then FIFO, exactly as specified. The queue does precisely what it was told. And the patron who waited concludes it is rigged, which is the perception the scheduler's own aging term exists to prevent. That gap was demonstrated with a failing test before anything was written, and the old behaviour is still pinned by a test so the change stays visible rather than becoming folklore.

Karaoke Eternal's singer rotation is the prior art: a singer with three songs queued does not get three turns in a row. Generalised from "singers" to "patrons with priority", the queue is dealt out one entry per patron per round. Crucially, rotation orders turns, not outcomes — the highest-scoring entry still plays first, or voting would stop meaning anything.

Two details would otherwise have been quiet bugs. Staff-pinned entries are held out of the rotation, or a patron would be interleaved between two pinned tracks and break the one override staff have. And an entry with no patron is treated as its own queue rather than merged — anonymous entries, like never-silent fallback tracks, would otherwise collapse into a single pseudo-patron taking one slot for all of them, starving the room of filler at exactly the wrong moment. It is on by default, because the default has to be the fair one: a venue opts out of patrons taking turns, not in.
Levelling the room, without ever making it clip. A jukebox queue is the worst case for loudness: a 2010 loudness-war master, a quiet Creative Commons home recording and a live guitar can follow one another inside four minutes. CON-6 normalises to −14 LUFS — the target Spotify and YouTube use, so a track sounds the same in the venue as it did in the patron's headphones on the way there.

The obvious implementation is gain = target − measured, and it is wrong. A track mastered to −6 LUFS gets −8 dB and is fine, but a quiet track with an untamed peak gets boosted into the ceiling and clips. That trade is not close. Uneven loudness is a comfort problem a human fixes with the master fader; digital clipping through a venue PA is audible distortion, and at volume it damages drivers. So gain is clamped by the track's true-peak headroom under a −1.0 dBTP ceiling — lossy codecs routinely reconstruct above 0 dBFS on decode, so a file that measures 0.0 dBFS is genuinely over after decoding. A peaky track plays slightly under target instead, and reports peakLimited so the console can show "−2.1 dB (peak-limited)" rather than leaving an operator to wonder why one track is quiet.

Three smaller decisions carry most of the correctness. The trim is written to pregain, never to volume — volume is the fader the DJ has a hand on, and software that fights a human hand loses. It is written for every track, including the 0 dB case, because otherwise the previous track's −8 dB silently leaks onto the next one. And an unmeasured track gets exactly 0 dB, never a guess: "we don't know" and "we measured it and it's fine" have to stay distinguishable, which is the same principle licenceClass follows. A live instrument gets an explicit soundcheck trim labelled as an operator setting, not a measurement, because a performance that hasn't happened cannot be analysed.
